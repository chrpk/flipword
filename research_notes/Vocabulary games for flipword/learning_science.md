# Learning science of vocabulary-practice game mechanics: what improves long-term word retention

Scope note: These notes cover the cognitive and SLA evidence base for ranking game mechanics. Sources were checked during this session (Nov 2026) unless marked **[foundational, not re-fetched]**. Those are classic studies cited from well-established literature with DOI links. Verify their exact numbers before quoting them publicly.

---

## 1. Retrieval practice / testing effect: recall vs recognition, productive vs receptive

### Takeaway
Retrieving a word beats re-reading it. This is the single most robust mechanic, with general testing-effect meta-analyses at g ≈ 0.5–0.6. Within retrieval, **recall (typing or saying the answer) beats recognition (choosing it)** for building the ability to produce and spell the word. **Productive retrieval (meaning → L2 word) builds productive knowledge.** Recognition is cheaper and faster per trial and helps lower-proficiency learners. The best-supported design is graduated difficulty: receptive recognition, then receptive recall, then productive recall, all with feedback.

### Cited Findings
- Nakata (2016, *IRAL* 54(3), 257–289) compared recognition, recall, hybrid (recall + recognition) and productive-recall-only formats. **Recall formats were more effective than recognition for acquiring productive knowledge of orthography (spelling).** Recognition formats were more desirable when spelling knowledge is not required, because they are more efficient. — [Nakata 2016, De Gruyter abstract](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)
- Kaitsu & Nakata (2025) evaluated flashcard apps against 24 research-based criteria and summarise the following:
  - Retrieval has four formats: receptive recognition, receptive recall, productive recognition and productive recall.
  - "Productive retrieval often yields greater productive knowledge than receptive retrieval" (citing Webb 2009 and others).
  - Receptive retrieval takes less time and effort.
  - Receptive retrieval was more effective than productive retrieval for lower-proficiency learners (Terai et al. 2021).
  - Recall often leads to better retention than recognition (Technique Feature Analysis), while recognition is more efficient (Nakata 2016).
  - Apps should increase difficulty for each item, receptive before productive and recognition before recall, following the desirable-difficulty framework (Suzuki et al. 2020: "effortful but successful retrieval enhances retention").
  - [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025, *CALL* (PDF)](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara (2020, *Modern Language Journal*) meta-analysed intentional vocabulary activities: 22 studies and 100 effect sizes covering flashcards, word lists, writing and fill-in-the-blanks.
  - Mean gains were about 60% on immediate meaning recall and 58% on immediate form recall.
  - Gains fell to **39.4% (meaning recall) and 25.1% (form recall) on delayed tests**.
  - Gains varied widely by activity, from 18.4% to 77.0% on immediate tests.
  - Direction of learning (L1→L2 vs L2→L1) affected learning.
  - [Webb et al. 2020, Cambridge listing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C); [x-mol abstract](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1341861690617110528)
- Kaitsu & Nakata summarise the same meta-analysis: **flashcards (i.e., retrieval with feedback) led to larger vocabulary gains than word lists, writing words in sentences or compositions, or fill-in-the-blanks.** — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- A Michigan State dissertation on L2→L1 paired-associate retrieval reports that **the benefits of effortful retrieval are conditional on retrieval success**. Longer lags increase effort but also failure. — [MSU dissertation](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/48649/OBJ/download)
- A 2024 *Heliyon* study (110 Chinese EFL students) found that retrieval practice limited to 2 rounds did **not** beat restudy. Adding a keyword mnemonic to retrieval did beat restudy. Retrieval needs enough repetitions to pay off. — [Heliyon 2024 / PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38317910)
- Kana-vocabulary study (L1 Chinese learners of Japanese): retrieval practice with corrective feedback improved 15-minute cued recall and **30- and 50-day recognition** compared with restudy. — [Behavioral Sciences 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16071124)
- Karatas et al. (2025, *Language Teaching Research*), Turkish university students: spaced retrieval with expanding gaps and feedback substantially outperformed a typical study-then-cram approach. — [Karatas et al. 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13621688211053525)
- **[foundational, not re-fetched]**
  - Karpicke & Roediger (2008, *Science*), Swahili–English word pairs: repeated retrieval produced roughly 80% one-week recall, versus about 35% when items were dropped from testing (but still restudied). — [doi:10.1126/science.1152408](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408)
  - Kang, Gollan & Pashler (2013, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*), "Don't just repeat after me": retrieval practice beat imitation (hear-and-repeat) for learning foreign (Hebrew) vocabulary. — [doi:10.3758/s13423-013-0450-z](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0450-z)
  - General testing-effect meta-analyses: Rowland (2014, *Psychological Bulletin*) g ≈ 0.50 ([doi:10.1037/a0037559](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)); Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, *Review of Educational Research*) g ≈ 0.61 ([doi:10.3102/0034654316689306](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306)).

### Inferences
- For flipword, **typed or spoken productive recall (meaning or picture or audio → L2 word)** should be the highest-value core mechanic. Recognition formats (MC, matching, T/F) are best used as on-ramps for new or weak items, or as fast fluency rounds. They should not be the main retention driver.
- The best-supported structure is a per-word difficulty ladder (recognition → receptive recall → productive recall). It also maps naturally onto game "levels" for a word.
- Because the benefit depends on retrieval success, hints and scaffolds (letter reveal, first letter, audio) are useful when they turn a failed retrieval into an effortful, successful one. They should be graded down in the review signal (see §8).

### Gaps
- I found no L2-specific meta-analysis that pools testing-effect sizes split by recall vs recognition, or by productive vs receptive retrieval format. Evidence is single studies plus TFA reasoning.
- I could not access the per-activity percentages in Webb et al. 2020, such as the exact flashcard vs fill-in-the-blank gains.

## 2. Generation, cloze/gap-fill, context, and task features (Involvement Load Hypothesis and Technique Feature Analysis)

### Takeaway
Of the ILH components, **evaluation** (deciding whether and how a word fits a context, comparing options) contributes most, followed by **need**. **Search** (looking a word up) adds essentially nothing. Technique Feature Analysis explains more variance than ILH and rewards motivation, noticing, retrieval, generative or creative use, and retention features: spacing, repetition, and linking form to meaning.

Gap-fill in sentences is useful for the "use" aspect and varied encounters. In the flashcard meta-analysis, however, it produced smaller gains than plain flashcard retrieval. Context-based games should therefore add retrieval to context; they should not replace retrieval with context.

### Cited Findings
- Yanagisawa & Webb (2021, *Language Learning*) meta-analysed ILH across 42 studies, 398 effect sizes and N = 4,628.
  - ILH explained **15.0% of variance on immediate and 5.1% on delayed posttests**.
  - **Evaluation contributed most, then need; search did not contribute.**
  - Test format and frequency of encounters moderated gains.
  - Involvement load mattered more than time on task.
  - [Yanagisawa & Webb 2021 summary](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/lang.12444); [BL record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100136951289_0x000001)
- Yanagisawa & Webb also proposed **"ILH Plus"**, an improved predictive model of incidental vocabulary learning, in *SSLA*. — [Cambridge PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5C5984B326F37FBF3A3DDA3C0EB2996C/S0272263121000577a.pdf/involvement-load-hypothesis-plus-creating-an-improved-predictive-model-of-incidental-vocabulary-learning.pdf)
- Liu & Reynolds (2022), a systematic review: the search component was the least present in vocabulary tasks. Other meta-analyses and conceptual papers report contradictory findings on ILH's predictive power. — [Behavioral Sciences / MDPI 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/12/10/354/html)
- Technique Feature Analysis (Nation & Webb 2011) is a checklist of 18 criteria grouped into **motivation, noticing, retrieval, creative (generative) use, and retention**.
  - Hu & Nassaji (2016, *System* 56, 28–39) found that TFA predicted learning better than ILH: it had greater explanatory power.
  - [Frontiers in Psychology 2024 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1306306/pdf); [Hu & Nassaji 2016 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_1850487269)
- A 2025 study applied ILH vs TFA to **digital game-based** vocabulary learning. It cautioned that relying only on TFA's total score may not produce the desired outcomes, because individual components matter. — [JRAL 2025 PDF](https://rals.scu.ac.ir/article_19303_6b021e9f03a8b5288ff8eb410d0346a6.pdf)
- Sudsa-ard (2023, Leeds PhD) found that **TFA components differ in how much they support short- versus long-term retention of word form**, using mixed-effects models. — [White Rose eThesis](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/34574/)
- On **varied encounters and use** (a TFA feature): meeting a partly known word in new contexts deepens knowledge, whether through a different meaning, collocation, inflection or part of speech. The degree of variation matters, so "round face" → "a round of drinks" is high variation. — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- Fill-in-the-blanks produced smaller gains than flashcards in the Webb et al. (2020) intentional-learning meta-analysis. — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025 summarising Webb et al. 2020](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- A 2026 *LTR* study of 85 Iranian EFL learners tested the "boundary conditions of retrieval-enhanced learning" by comparing retrieval with contextual inference for multi-word expressions. I could not retrieve its results. — [Tadayonifar & Entezari 2026](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13621688261452314)
- **[foundational, not re-fetched]** Laufer & Hulstijn (2001, *Applied Linguistics* 22(1)) introduced ILH with three components: need, search and evaluation. Tasks with higher involvement load, such as writing a sentence with the word or choosing the right word for a gap, should produce better retention than reading with glosses. — [doi:10.1093/applin/22.1.1](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/22.1.1)

### Inferences
- The highest-value context game is **"choose or produce the word that fits this sentence"**, using the card's example sentence with the target blanked out. It combines evaluation (strong ILH component), retrieval and context. Typed production (cloze recall) should beat MC cloze for form learning.
- Evaluation can also be added cheaply to existing formats. For example, a sentence-level T/F asks "Does *word* fit this sentence?" instead of "Does this meaning belong to this word?".
- "Generative or creative use" (TFA) suggests a "use it" mechanic: build a sentence from word tiles that includes the target, or reorder a scrambled example sentence. This is higher-load but slower, so it suits mastered words rather than new ones.
- Cloze should complement flashcard-style recall, not replace it. The meta-analytic gains for fill-in-the-blank were lower than for flashcards.

### Gaps
- I found no recent effect sizes isolating typed cloze vs MC cloze in digital apps.
- Effect sizes for the individual TFA components are not available in sources I could access.

## 3. Desirable difficulties: spacing, interleaving, retrieval effort, feedback timing, pretesting / errorful generation

### Takeaway
Spacing is the best-quantified L2 effect: a medium-to-large meta-analytic effect, with **longer spacing winning on delayed tests**. Expanding schedules have no clear advantage over equal-interval schedules. FSRS already handles spacing, so in-game spacing (across rounds and sessions) matters more than extra within-session repetitions, which show diminishing returns.

Feedback is essential, but its timing (immediate vs delayed) matters little. **Guess-first (pretesting) with feedback** reliably helps even for brand-new L2 words, including word–picture formats, with d ≈ 0.2–0.67. Semantic clustering (themed sets of near-synonyms or same-category words) causes short-term interference, though long-term harm is less certain.

### Cited Findings
- Kim & Webb (2022, *Language Learning* 72(1), 269–319) meta-analysed spacing: **48 experiments, 98 effect sizes, N = 3,411.**
  - Spaced practice had a **medium-to-large effect** on L2 learning.
  - Shorter spacing was as effective as longer spacing on immediate posttests, but **less effective on delayed posttests**.
  - Secondary sources describe the benefit as most robust for vocabulary.
  - [SSLLT summary](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/35602); [BL record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100152978253_0x000001)
- The thesis version of Kim & Webb reports that variability in spacing effects was explained by the learning target, number of sessions, type of practice, activity type, feedback timing and retention interval. The effect depended on whether retrieval practice was provided and whether spacing was within or between sessions. — [Western University ETD 8600](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8600/)
- **Expanding spacing:** recent research, including Kim & Webb 2022, suggests it does not necessarily beat other schedules, despite TFA listing it as beneficial. — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- **Block size**, the number of items studied at once, gives mixed results. Larger blocks did better in Nakata & Suzuki 2019 and Nakata et al. 2023. Nakata & Webb (2016) found little effect once temporal spacing is controlled. Larger blocks mainly work because they create more spacing between repetitions of the same item. — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- **Within-session repetitions:** Cao et al. (2025, CogSci) found that as retrievals rose from 5 to 10, gains on 25-minute retention tests diminished. Spacing also costs more total time, so there is a trade-off. — [CogSci 2025 PDF](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/49545/galley/37507/download)
- Nakata (2017, *SSLA*), "Does repeated practice make perfect? The effects of within-session repeated retrieval on second language vocabulary learning", is the key L2 study on within-session repetitions. I could not access its detailed numbers. — [Cambridge SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/does-repeated-practice-make-perfect-the-effects-of-withinsession-repeated-retrieval-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/F14BA8A576CD2563D14CEA46E35D842E)
- **Feedback timing:** in Nakata's flashcard research, immediate and delayed feedback were similarly effective, and learners perceived them as equally effective. — [Nakata 2013 dissertation](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265381966_Optimising_second_language_vocabulary_learning_from_flashcards); [VLI 4(1) Nakata 2015](https://vli-journal.org/wp/vli-41-nakata-2015/)
- **Retirement criteria** (when to stop drilling an item) give conflicting evidence. Dropout schedules helped in Pyc & Rawson 2007 but not in Kornell & Bjork 2008 or Pyc & Rawson 2011. — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- **Interference / semantic clustering:** learning semantically related words together, such as inu/neko ("dog"/"cat"), risks cross-association errors.
  - Earlier studies found negative effects (Papathanasiou 2009; Wilcox & Medina 2013).
  - More recent work suggests effects are not robust long-term (Kemp & McDonald 2021; Nakata & Suzuki 2019), but may still hurt short-term (Bach & Barclay 2025).
  - [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- **Pretesting / errorful generation:**
  - Potts & Shanks (2014, *JEP: General*) found that guessing the meaning of rare English words or Basque (Euskara) words before seeing the answer improved later memory, even when the guesses were wrong.
  - Learners judged guessing to be *less* effective than reading. This is a metacognitive illusion.
  - Follow-up work locates the benefit in **enhanced processing of the feedback**.
  - [UCL eprint](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1399515/1/RPottsLastRevision.pdf); [Potts, Davies & Shanks](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10052994/1/Shanks_Errorful%20generation%20FINAL.pdf)
- **Chua & Pan (2026, *Cognitive Research: Principles & Implications* 11:20), "Duolingo-inspired pretesting with words and pictures"**: 4 experiments with adult online learners who had no Spanish.
  - Learners guessed by multiple choice (word→image or image→word) and then received feedback, compared with reading the pairs.
  - Pretesting improved later **cued recall (d = 0.18–0.40)** and, in most cases, **MC tests (d = 0.25–0.67)**.
  - Participants preferred pretesting.
  - The authors note that earlier L2 pretesting effects were limited or absent for L2 translations when learners lack prior knowledge (Kang et al. 2013; Potts & Shanks 2014).
  - Retention was tested in the same session after a short distractor task, so long-delay evidence is lacking.
  - [Chua & Pan 2026 PDF](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/CP_2026.pdf)
- **Time limits and desirable difficulty:** Benjamin & Bjork (2000) found that time pressure at retrieval disproportionately hurts access to elaborately encoded memories compared with rote-encoded ones. — [Benjamin & Bjork 2000 PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Benjamin_RBjork_2000.pdf)

### Inferences
- **"Guess first" introductions are evidence-backed and game-friendly.** A new word appears as an MC guess with its photo or translation, then feedback. This is a cheap win for flipword's introduction of new cards. It is effective even with wrong guesses, provided the correct answer is shown clearly afterwards.
- Games should **interleave and space repetitions within a round**: show other items between repeats of the same item, rather than repeating an item back-to-back. They should also avoid drawing all items from one tight semantic set when words are new. Themed sets are fine for review of known words.
- Immediate feedback is fine (timing is neutral), so games need not delay feedback for learning reasons.
- Repeating the same item 8–10 times within one game session has diminishing returns. It is better to let FSRS schedule the next encounter.

### Gaps
- Long-delay (≥1 week) evidence for pretesting with L2 word–picture pairs is lacking. Chua & Pan tested the same day.
- I found no L2 study isolating interleaving of task formats, as opposed to interleaving items.

## 4. Multimodal / dual coding: pictures, audio, listening, dictation, pronunciation and speaking (production effect)

### Takeaway
Pictures help when learners actually retrieve from them, but can produce **overconfidence** when merely viewed. Picture-cued retrieval is the right use of card photos. **Saying words aloud (the production effect) improves memory, and the effect may be larger in L2 than in L1.** Retrieval still beats mere repetition ("repeat after me"), so the strongest speaking mechanic is *spoken recall*: see a picture or meaning, then say the L2 word. Automatic speech recognition (ASR) feedback helps learners acquire spoken forms. Listening-based tasks train the phonological form, which flipword's text-only games currently ignore.

### Cited Findings
- Carpenter & Olson (2012, *JEP: LMC* 38(1), 92–101), Swahili words:
  - Pictures were initially *not* better than English translations, because learners were overconfident with pictures.
  - When overconfidence was removed through retrieval practice or warnings, **words were learned better from pictures than from translations**.
  - [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/is-a-picture-really-worth-a-thousand-words-evaluating-contributio/)
- A 2022 Groningen thesis could not replicate a picture advantage in an adaptive learning system. Pictures and translations were equally adequate, so the picture advantage is mixed. — [Groningen thesis PDF](https://gmwpublic.studenttheses.ub.rug.nl/445/1/Thesis_AF_Aleman.pdf)
- Chua & Pan (2026): word→image and image→word MC guessing with feedback beat reading (d up to 0.67 on MC tests). — [Chua & Pan 2026](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/CP_2026.pdf)
- **Production effect in L2:** Brown & Roembke (2024, *Memory & Cognition*) found that unbalanced German–English bilinguals recognised spoken words better than silently read ones in both languages. **The production effect was larger in the L2.** The authors attribute this to more phonological encoding and/or effort. — [PMC11111515](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11111515/)
- *Language Teaching Research* (2022): Hebrew speakers learned Esperanto words with vs without vocal production, including delayed testing. — ["The effect of vocal production on vocabulary learning in a second language"](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/the-effect-of-vocal-production-on-vocabulary-learning-in-a-second/)
- *SSLLT* (2026), collocations: reading aloud and reading-while-listening both produced more collocation learning than silent reading. — [SSLLT PDF](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/download/43494/41362/126431)
- A child pilot with Modern Standard Arabic (72 English-speaking children) found minimal production-effect differences. The evidence for children is mixed. — [SDL repository](https://drepo.sdl.edu.sa/signposting/describedby/f69ba55e-5587-44e6-911c-c4b468a17049)
- **ASR:** Bashori et al. (2024) found that ASR pronunciation feedback facilitates acquisition of the spoken forms of L2 words. Kaitsu & Nakata list ASR as a desirable flashcard-app feature. — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- **[foundational, not re-fetched]** Kang, Gollan & Pashler (2013): retrieval (producing the word from a picture cue) beat imitation (repeating a word just heard) for foreign vocabulary. Mere repeat-after-me is weaker than spoken recall. — [doi:10.3758/s13423-013-0450-z](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0450-z)
- **[foundational, not re-fetched]** MacLeod et al. (2010, *JEP: LMC*) defined the production effect: words read aloud are remembered better than words read silently. The benefit is mainly relative to silently read items in the same list. — [doi:10.1037/a0018785](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018785)

### Inferences
- High-value multimodal mechanics for flipword:
  - **Photo → type or say the L2 word** (picture-cued productive recall).
  - **Listen → pick or type the meaning** (audio-only receptive recall, which trains the spoken form).
  - **Listen → type the word** (dictation, which links sound and spelling).
  - **"Say it" rounds** in which the learner speaks the L2 word, ideally checked with the Web Speech API's speech recognition where available, or self-graded where not.
- Display photos as *cues to retrieve from*, not as decoration on the answer side. This exploits the picture advantage and avoids the Carpenter & Olson overconfidence trap.
- Browser speech recognition coverage varies (Chrome/Safari support differs), so self-grading is a necessary fallback. This is a technical inference, not from the learning-science sources.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analytic effect size for dictation or audio-cued recall specifically in L2 vocabulary.
- I could not retrieve effect sizes for the Esperanto vocal-production study or for Bashori et al. 2024.

## 5. Collocations, word families, semantic mapping and morphology

### Takeaway
The evidence base here is thinner for app or game formats. Varied encounters with different collocations and meanings deepen knowledge, according to TFA and Webb & Nation. Morphology and word parts are recommended content in flashcard frameworks. **Semantic grouping of new words carries short-term interference risk**, while thematic grouping of known words is less risky. Collocation and word-family games are best treated as "depth" extensions for already-learned words.

### Cited Findings
- Varied encounters and use, through new collocations, meanings and inflections, deepen word knowledge, and greater variation means more learning (Webb & Nation 2017; Nation & Webb 2011). — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- Recommended flashcard information types include audio, phonetic symbols, images, video, parts of speech, **word parts**, and example sentences or phrases. Learning strategies include contextual cues, semantic associations, word parts and mnemonics. — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- Semantic clustering effects: see §3. They range from negative early findings to weak long-term effects in recent work, with possible short-term harm. — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- Reading aloud and reading-while-listening improved collocation learning over silent reading. — [SSLLT 2026](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/download/43494/41362/126431)
- The ReCALL 2026 mobile meta-analysis found that apps targeting form + meaning had d = 1.33. Apps targeting **form + meaning + use had a smaller d = 0.65**, perhaps because "use" outcomes are harder to move or are measured more stringently. — [Zhou & Zhou 2026, ReCALL](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/79C8F00E48D521ED3BCFE0625B5549F3/S0958344025100335a.pdf/metaanalysis_on_mobileassisted_vocabulary_learning_do_mobile_applications_help.pdf)

### Inferences
- flipword cards currently hold only a word, a translation, an optional sentence and a photo. Collocation and word-family games would need extra data, either user-entered or generated. The main research-backed low-cost move is **using the example sentence for varied-context cloze**.
- Avoid putting near-synonyms or same-category words from a fresh batch as MC distractors for new words. That builds the interference the literature warns about. Use them as distractors only once words are established, where fine discrimination is an evaluation task.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis or strong controlled study of collocation or word-family *games* in apps. The evidence comes from frameworks and reading studies.
- Semantic-mapping game evidence was not found in this session.

## 6. Meta-analyses of digital game-based and technology-assisted vocabulary learning: effect sizes and moderators

### Takeaway
Games and apps beat comparison conditions with moderate-to-large effects (d ≈ 0.5–1.4). These numbers are inflated by weak control conditions, novelty, short studies and publication bias, and **few studies test delayed retention**. Game design features moderate effects. Adventure or challenge-oriented designs beat simple drill games in one meta-analysis. Out-of-class, longer-duration use shows bigger effects. No meta-analysis I found isolates competition, time pressure or adaptivity as moderators of vocabulary retention.

### Cited Findings
- **Zhou & Zhou (2026, *ReCALL* 38(1), 75–93)**, Bayesian meta-analysis of 65 mobile-app vocabulary studies (2010–2024):
  - Overall **d = 1.28** (95% CI .99–1.66 in Table 2, reported as 1.03–1.52 in the text) for treatments of 10 weeks or more.
  - **Only 11 studies reported delayed posttests: d = 1.41 [.86, 1.95].**
  - Receptive outcomes d = 1.38, productive d = 1.25, mixed d = 0.66.
  - Out-of-class d = 1.42 vs classroom d = 0.82.
  - Educational apps d = 1.31 vs general apps d = 1.08.
  - Intermediate learners d = 1.57, elementary d = 0.79 (CI crosses 0).
  - After adjusting for **publication bias**, effects dropped to medium: receptive 0.87, productive 1.46, mixed 0.68.
  - [ReCALL PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/79C8F00E48D521ED3BCFE0625B5549F3/S0958344025100335a.pdf/metaanalysis_on_mobileassisted_vocabulary_learning_do_mobile_applications_help.pdf)
- The same paper's table of prior meta-analyses lists the following. — [Zhou & Zhou 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/79C8F00E48D521ED3BCFE0625B5549F3/S0958344025100335a.pdf/metaanalysis_on_mobileassisted_vocabulary_learning_do_mobile_applications_help.pdf)
  - Yu & Trainin 2022: 34 studies, N = 2,511, d = 0.64.
  - Mahdi 2018: 16 studies, d = 0.67.
  - **Hao et al. 2021: 45 studies, N = 2,374, d = 0.845**, with moderators including devices, games and study setting.
- Yu & Trainin (2022, *ReCALL*): technology-assisted L2 vocabulary learning d = 0.64, with significant heterogeneity. The benefit was larger when the target language is close to the L1. — [Yu & Trainin 2022](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1519&context=teachlearnfacpub)
- **Chen, Tseng & Hsiao (2018, *BJET* 49(1), 69–77)**, digital game-based vocabulary learning:
  - **Game design features significantly moderated effects** (Q = 5.857, p = .016).
  - Age and linguistic background did not (p = .34, p = .99).
  - Using flow theory, the authors argue that adventure-oriented games sit higher on the "challenge" axis than non-adventure games.
  - [dblp/BJET record](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/bjet/ChenTH18.html); [OpenDevEd library](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/24BG25I9)
- Tsai & Tsai (2018, *Computers & Education* 125), 26 studies: the largest effects came from **game vs alternative-activity** comparisons. Comparisons that grouped players by a non-game variable were non-significant. This shows design and control choices drive the effect size. — [NTNU record](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/digital-game-based-second-language-vocabulary-learning-and-condit-2/)
- Kao (2014), 25 EFL studies: DGBL d = 0.695. Dixon, Dixon & Jordan (2022), L2 gains: between-groups d = 0.50, within-group d = 0.95, with moderators including developer purpose and game design features. — [search summary of HKIER / GSU records](https://hkier.cuhk.edu.hk/en/publications/ej-v42n2-113-141); [GSU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/alesl_facpub/2)
- A systematic review of DGBLL (2010–2025) found that **delayed posttests are comparatively rare**, and that novelty and time-on-task effects are hard to rule out. — [ResearchGate: "Beyond scores" review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398514543_Beyond_scores_a_systematic_review_of_learning_and_psychological_outcomes_of_digital_game-based_language_learning_2010-2025)
- A 2025 Bayesian DGBL vocabulary meta-analysis *protocol* plans moderators for game design, assessment type, game source and intervention length. Competition, time pressure and adaptivity are **not** coded. — [Protocol](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391617162_Protocol_for_Bayesian_Meta-analysis_on_Digital_Game-Enhanced_Vocabulary_Learning)

### Inferences
- Meta-analytic "games work" effects mostly compare games with weak baselines such as word lists or traditional classes. They do **not** tell us that a timed match game beats plain flashcard recall. In the controlled cognitive literature, the mechanisms inside the game (retrieval type, spacing, feedback) are what predict retention.
- The moderator pattern (design features matter, out-of-class and longer use larger) suggests that **sustained voluntary use** is a major pathway. Engagement features earn their place by increasing the number of spaced retrievals done, not by improving learning per trial.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis coding competition, time pressure, narrative or adaptivity as moderators of *vocabulary retention*.
- I could not obtain Chen et al. 2018's overall effect size or study count.
- Liu et al. (2025, *CALL*) on mobile game-based language learning (DOI 10.1080/09588221.2025.2528786) was not accessed, so its genre moderators are unknown.

## 7. Time pressure and gamification (points, streaks): retention or only engagement?

### Takeaway
Gamification has a small-to-medium positive effect on cognitive learning outcomes (g ≈ 0.49), robust in rigorous studies. Its effects on motivation and behaviour are smaller and less stable. Social and fiction elements and "competition + collaboration" moderate behavioural outcomes.

**Time pressure has no direct evidence of helping vocabulary *acquisition*.** It is recommended in frameworks for *fluency* (speed of access) on already-known words. Cognitive evidence suggests that speed pressure impairs access to more deeply encoded knowledge. Best practice: untimed or generous timing for learning-phase retrieval, and timed rounds only for known or mastered words as fluency training.

### Cited Findings
- **Sailer & Homner (2020, *Educational Psychology Review* 32(1), 77–112):** gamification of learning had significant small effects:
  - cognitive **g = 0.49** (k = 19, N = 1,686)
  - motivational g = 0.36 (k = 16, N = 2,246)
  - behavioural g ≈ 0.25 (k = 9, N = 951; the behavioural figure comes from secondary records)
  - The cognitive effect was stable in high-rigor studies; motivational and behavioural effects were less stable.
  - **Game fiction and social interaction moderated behavioural outcomes, and combining competition with collaboration was particularly effective.**
  - [Uni Augsburg OPUS](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056); [gamification-research.org summary](https://gamification-research.org/2019/08/does-gamification-of-learning-work/)
- **Fluency development** is one of Nation's four strands. Kaitsu & Nakata recommend it in flashcard apps "by imposing a time limit on retrieval practice", for known items. — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- Benjamin & Bjork (2000): time pressure at recognition disproportionately impairs access to elaboratively rehearsed items compared with rote ones. — [Benjamin & Bjork 2000](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Benjamin_RBjork_2000.pdf)
- The ILH meta-analysis found that involvement load predicted learning better than time on task. Spending *more* time is not what drives learning, so fast games are not inherently worse if the processing is deep. — [Yanagisawa & Webb 2021](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/lang.12444)
- In Chua & Pan's study, learners *preferred* guessing-based activities. In Potts & Shanks's, learners misjudged guessing as worse than reading. Learner preference and learning value can diverge either way. — [Chua & Pan 2026](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/CP_2026.pdf); [Potts & Shanks](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1399515/1/RPottsLastRevision.pdf)

### Inferences
- Points, streaks and leaderboards are unlikely to change retention per trial. Their value is in driving **more frequent spaced sessions**, which spacing research says matters a lot. Streaks aligned with FSRS due reviews (finish your reviews today) direct motivation toward the effective behaviour.
- Speed rounds (Match, True or false) should draw from words FSRS considers known (stability above some threshold), so time pressure builds fluency rather than interrupting encoding of new words.
- Optional cooperative or social modes may beat pure competition, per the Sailer & Homner moderator. This is weak evidence for vocabulary specifically.

### Gaps
- **I found no controlled study directly comparing timed vs untimed vocabulary games on delayed retention.** This is a clear gap.
- No vocabulary-specific gamification meta-analysis separating points, badges and streaks was found.
- Duolingo's internal studies were not reviewed (peer review status varies).

## 8. How should game results feed spaced repetition?

### Takeaway
There is no direct empirical study of "use game answers as SRS review signals". The principles imply three rules.
1. Successful *recall* (productive or receptive) is strong evidence of retrievability, while *recognition* or speeded verification is weaker evidence and partly guessable.
2. A retrieval done in a game is itself a learning event that changes memory, so it should be logged as a review, with appropriate weighting, rather than ignored.
3. Games should prioritise due or weak items (adaptive sequencing) rather than random cards.

### Cited Findings
- **Adaptive sequencing**, adjusting practice schedules from learner performance, is a recommended feature because it focuses practice on difficult or unknown items (Nakata 2020). — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)
- Spacing benefits are largest on delayed tests when spacing is longer. Within-session extra retrievals give diminishing returns. — [Kim & Webb 2022 summary](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/35602); [Cao et al. 2025](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/49545/galley/37507/download)
- Recognition formats are more guessable and less demanding than recall; recall builds form (spelling) knowledge that recognition does not. — [Nakata 2016](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)
- Errors with feedback still produce learning (pretesting). A wrong answer in a game is not just a lapse; it is an encoding opportunity, provided feedback is shown. — [Chua & Pan 2026](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/CP_2026.pdf)
- Retirement and dropout criteria have mixed evidence. — [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)

### Inferences
These are design recommendations, not empirically tested.
- **Map format to signal strength:**
  - typed productive recall correct (no hints) → "Good" or "Easy" by latency
  - productive recall with hints, or unscramble → "Hard"
  - MC or matching correct → at most "Hard"/"Good", with low weight, or only update if the card is not yet due
  - any wrong answer → "Again"
  - T/F correct → too guessable (50%) to count as a success signal, but a wrong answer is informative
- Avoid double-counting multiple retrievals of the same card within one game session. Use the first attempt, since the testing literature treats the first retrieval after a delay as the diagnostic one; the others are massed.
- Feeding recognition successes as full "Good" reviews would push intervals out on words the learner cannot produce. That risks premature retirement and inflated FSRS stability.
- Games should pull a mix of due cards and recent lapses, and avoid same-category sets for new words.

### Gaps
- I found no published research validating FSRS-style ratings derived from game formats (MC vs typed vs speeded) or calibrating recognition-based signals against later recall. This would need flipword's own data, for example by comparing next-review recall after game-logged vs flashcard-logged reviews.

## 9. How flipword's current 5 games score on these principles, and what they miss

### Takeaway
flipword's games lean heavily on **recognition and verification** (Match, Quiz, True or false) plus two **form-focused productive tasks** (Spell it, Unscramble). Only Spell it delivers the highest-value mechanic: unscaffolded productive recall. None uses the example sentence (context, evaluation, varied encounters), the photo as a retrieval cue, audio-only listening, speaking or the production effect, or pretesting for new words. Two use time pressure, which is appropriate only for known words.

### Cited Findings
The scoring basis, with each criterion's source:

| Criterion | Source |
|---|---|
| Retrieval > presentation; recall > recognition for retention and form | [Nakata 2016](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en); [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf) |
| Productive retrieval builds productive knowledge; receptive suits low proficiency | [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf) |
| Evaluation strongest ILH component | [Yanagisawa & Webb 2021](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/lang.12444) |
| Spacing, longer is better for delayed retention | [Kim & Webb 2022](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/35602) |
| Pretesting with words and pictures helps | [Chua & Pan 2026](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/CP_2026.pdf) |
| Production effect larger in L2 | [Brown & Roembke 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11111515/) |
| Pictures help when retrieval removes overconfidence | [Carpenter & Olson 2012](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/is-a-picture-really-worth-a-thousand-words-evaluating-contributio/) |
| Time limits are for fluency | [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf) |

### Inferences
Assessment of each game. The ratings are my judgement from the principles above, scored High/Med/Low for long-term retention value.

- **Match (tap word–meaning pairs, timed). Retention value: Low–Medium.**
  - It is receptive or productive *recognition*.
  - Process of elimination makes later pairs nearly free, so effortful retrieval is low.
  - Time pressure suits fluency on known words only.
  - All pairs are visible at once, so it is closer to re-exposure than retrieval for the last few pairs.
  - Good as a warm-up or fluency game.
  - Fixes:
    - Hide pairs that are already found and refill the board from a queue, so the last items aren't free.
    - Use audio or photo tiles for cross-modal matching.
    - Draw only from known words when timed.
- **Quiz (4-option MC, both directions). Retention value: Medium.**
  - It is recognition in both directions, which is good, and a sound format for **pretesting new words** (Chua & Pan).
  - It is weak for form or spelling learning (Nakata 2016) and is guessable.
  - Distractors from the same semantic set may cause interference for new words.
  - Fixes:
    - Use it as stage 1 of a per-word ladder.
    - Add photo→word and audio→meaning variants.
    - Use sentence-cloze MC, which adds evaluation.
- **True or false (60 s speed round). Retention value: Low.**
  - Verification is the shallowest retrieval: a 50% guess rate and no generation.
  - Time pressure is added on top.
  - Showing wrong word–meaning pairings risks encoding false associations. This is an inference; I found no direct L2 study.
  - Best repurposed as a fluency round for mastered words, or as sentence-level evaluation ("Does this word fit this sentence?").
- **Spell it (meaning → type the word, optional audio). Retention value: High.**
  - Productive recall builds form and spelling knowledge, the best-supported format for production (Nakata 2016; Webb et al. 2020).
  - Missing:
    - a photo-cue variant
    - an audio-only dictation variant
    - a sentence-cloze variant (type the missing word in the example sentence)
    - graded hints that keep retrieval effortful-but-successful
- **Unscramble (rebuild the word from letters, with hints). Retention value: Medium.**
  - Productive *recognition* of form: the letters are given, so the task is reconstruction rather than recall.
  - It is lower effort than Spell it, but a useful scaffold between recognition and full recall, especially for longer or orthographically hard words.
  - Score it as "Hard" at most in FSRS.

**What the five games miss, ranked by expected learning value:**
1. **Context and evaluation:** sentence cloze using the card's example sentence, as MC first and typed later. This brings varied encounters, evaluation and retrieval together.
2. **Spoken production:** a "say it" game (photo or meaning → speak the L2 word), with speech recognition if available or else self-check. It exploits the production effect and retrieval, and builds the spoken form.
3. **Listening:** audio-only → meaning (receptive) and audio → type (dictation). These train the phonological form, which text games ignore.
4. **Photo-cued retrieval:** picture → L2 word, typed or chosen. It uses existing card photos as cues.
5. **Pretesting / guess-first introduction** of new cards with feedback.
6. **A per-word difficulty ladder and adaptive selection** feeding FSRS: recognition → recall → production → speeded fluency.
7. **Generative use** for mastered words: build or reorder a sentence containing the word, or a word-family/collocation pick. Its value is evidenced in frameworks (TFA creative use), but there are no strong game-specific RCTs.

### Gaps
- No study evaluates these exact game formats head-to-head on delayed retention. The ranking is a principled synthesis, not a measured comparison.
- Effects of Match-style elimination and of T/F false-pair exposure on L2 vocabulary were not directly tested in any source found. flipword could A/B test them with its own delayed-review data.
