# Turn flipword's games into recall engines

flipword's next games should make the learner **produce** the word, in context, from sound, from a picture and out loud. The five current games mostly ask the learner to **recognize** it. The best-supported finding in vocabulary research is that effortful retrieval with feedback beats re-exposure, and that recall (typing or saying the word) builds productive and spelling knowledge that recognition does not. Three of flipword's five games (Match, Quiz, True or false) are recognition or verification, and only Spell it delivers unscaffolded productive recall. None of the five uses the example sentence, the photo, listening without text, or speaking, even though cards already carry most of that data. The recommended build order is a sentence-gap game (**Gap Line**), a listening game (**Echo**), a photo-cued recall game (**Snapshot**) and a say-it-aloud game (**Out Loud**, self-checked first). Next come two arcade wrappers around typed recall: a falling-word typing game (**Downpour**) and a boss battle that climbs from recognition to recall (**Showdown**). The fun has to come from making recall itself the action, rewarded with informative feedback and personal bests, not from timers, coins or luck. Market evidence warns that typed-recall arcade games draw less voluntary play than tap games, so engagement needs deliberate design. Before any of this, three repo issues need fixing: language-code matching in `src/lib/speech.ts`, the missing `media-src` in the CSP, and the `microphone=()` Permissions-Policy, which blocks speech input. Five system-wide changes would raise the value of every game, new and old: show the right answer after every wrong one, offer a one-tap retry of missed words, pick due or weak cards instead of random ones, score accuracy over speed, and let typed-recall games (only those) feed FSRS on an opt-in basis.

## Three of the five games test recognition, the weakest learning signal

Retrieval practice is the most robust mechanic in learning science. Classic testing-effect meta-analyses put it at **g ≈ 0.50–0.61** over restudy ([Rowland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559); [Adesope et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306)). Within retrieval, the format matters. Nakata found that **recall formats beat recognition for acquiring the written form of words**, while recognition is more efficient when spelling is not the goal ([Nakata 2016](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)). A 2025 review of flashcard apps against 24 research criteria concludes that productive retrieval (meaning → L2 word) yields more productive knowledge, but that receptive retrieval suits lower-proficiency learners. It recommends that apps move each item from recognition to recall and from receptive to productive, because "effortful but successful retrieval enhances retention" ([Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)). The caveat that shapes game design is that **the benefit depends on success**. Effortful retrievals that fail teach less ([MSU dissertation](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/48649/OBJ/download)), and two rounds of retrieval alone did not beat restudy in one 2024 study ([Heliyon 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38317910)). Games need hints that turn a failed recall into a hard-won success, and they need enough repetitions across sessions.

Delayed retention is where the gap shows. In a meta-analysis of intentional vocabulary activities, mean gains fell from about 60% on immediate meaning recall to **39.4% (meaning recall) and 25.1% (form recall) on delayed tests**. Flashcard-style retrieval with feedback beat word lists, sentence writing and fill-in-the-blank ([Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C), as summarized by [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)). Form recall decays fastest. That is exactly the knowledge recognition games don't train.

The code agrees with that analysis. `shared/games.ts` already labels Match, Quiz and True or false as `recognition` and Spell it and Unscramble as `recall`. Unscramble hands the learner the letters, though, so it reconstructs the form rather than recalling it. The table scores each game's long-term retention value using the principles above. The ratings are a principled synthesis; no study has compared these formats head to head.

| Game | What the learner actually does | Retention value | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spell it | Sees meaning, types the word (productive recall) | **High** | No hint ladder; no photo, audio-only or sentence prompts |
| Unscramble | Rebuilds the word from given letters | Medium | Can be solved as a letter puzzle; it's a scaffold, not recall |
| Quiz | Picks 1 of 4 in either direction | Medium | Guessable; random distractors; weak for spelling |
| Match | Pairs 6 word/meaning tiles against the clock | Low–Medium | The last pairs come free by elimination; speed rewards visual cues |
| True or false | Judges word = meaning pairings for 60 s | **Low** | 50% guess rate; a wrong answer gets only a 250 ms colour flash, with no correction |

Two of those weaknesses are visible in the code. In `TrueFalseGame.tsx`, a wrong answer sets a red flash for 250 ms and moves straight to the next statement, so the learner never sees the correct pairing after reading a false one like "chien = cat". MC lures carry a documented **negative-suggestibility** risk: learners later reproduce wrong options they saw, and corrective feedback reduces this. For children's vocabulary, retrieval practice helped only with feedback ([van Eersel dissertation](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/105295/Proefschrift_GvEersel.pdf)). Second, `worker/routes/games.ts` draws cards with `order by random()`. Games therefore ignore which words are due, weak or recently missed, which wastes the adaptive sequencing the review recommends ([Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)).

## Context, sound, pictures and speech are the untapped mechanics

The biggest gaps are four channels that the card data or the browser already provide but no game uses.

**The example sentence enables evaluation, the strongest component of task involvement.** A meta-analysis of the Involvement Load Hypothesis (42 studies, 398 effect sizes, N = 4,628) found that **evaluation contributed most to learning**, followed by need, while search added nothing. Evaluation means deciding whether a word fits a context. Involvement load predicted learning better than time on task ([Yanagisawa & Webb 2021](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/lang.12444)). Meeting a word in a new context also deepens knowledge, because the variation itself teaches. One caveat matters for ranking: plain fill-in-the-blank produced **smaller gains than flashcard retrieval** in the intentional-learning meta-analysis ([Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)). A sentence game should add retrieval to context, with the learner producing the blanked word. It should not replace recall with reading.

**Pictures help when they are retrieval cues, not decoration.** Learners shown Swahili words with pictures were initially overconfident and learned no better than from translations. Once retrieval practice removed the overconfidence, **pictures beat translations** ([Carpenter & Olson 2012](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/is-a-picture-really-worth-a-thousand-words-evaluating-contributio/)). A 2022 thesis found no picture advantage in an adaptive system, so the effect is real but mixed ([Groningen thesis](https://gmwpublic.studenttheses.ub.rug.nl/445/1/Thesis_AF_Aleman.pdf)). The design rule is to put the photo on the question side and make the learner produce the word from it.

**Guessing before learning works, even when the guess is wrong.** In four experiments with adults who knew no Spanish, guessing word→image or image→word by multiple choice and then getting feedback improved later **cued recall (d = 0.18–0.40) and MC tests (d = 0.25–0.67)** compared with reading the pairs. Participants also preferred it ([Chua & Pan 2026](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/CP_2026.pdf)). Earlier work found that learners judge guessing *less* effective than reading, a metacognitive illusion, and that the benefit comes from processing the feedback more deeply ([Potts & Shanks](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1399515/1/RPottsLastRevision.pdf)). Chua & Pan tested in the same session, so the long-delay evidence is still missing. This turns Quiz into a legitimate first-encounter tool for new cards, provided the correct answer is always revealed.

**Saying the word aloud adds a memory boost that appears larger in a second language.** Unbalanced German–English bilinguals recognized words they had spoken better than words read silently, and **the production effect was larger in their L2** ([Brown & Roembke 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11111515/)). Retrieval still beats imitation. Producing a foreign word from a picture cue outperformed repeating a word just heard ([Kang, Gollan & Pashler 2013](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0450-z)). The strongest speaking mechanic is therefore *spoken recall*: see the photo or meaning, then say the L2 word. Repeat-after-me is weaker. Evidence for children is mixed. Listening-based tasks (hear the word, then pick the meaning or type it) train the sound-to-form link that flipword's text-only games ignore. They have sound mechanistic support, but no format-specific meta-analysis was found.

Spacing and time pressure set the boundaries for all of these. The spacing meta-analysis (48 experiments, N = 3,411) found a **medium-to-large effect**, with longer spacing winning on delayed tests ([Kim & Webb 2022](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/35602)). Raising within-session retrievals from 5 to 10 gave diminishing returns ([Cao et al. 2025](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/49545/galley/37507/download)). Games should therefore space repeats inside a round and leave the long gaps to FSRS rather than drilling one word ten times. No controlled study compares timed and untimed vocabulary games on delayed retention. Time pressure does impair access to deeply encoded memories more than rote ones ([Benjamin & Bjork 2000](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Benjamin_RBjork_2000.pdf)). Frameworks recommend timed retrieval only for building fluency on words the learner already knows ([Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)). Match and True or false should draw from known words.

The market is moving the same way. The newest feature from the largest app shows an image and asks the learner to say the word aloud with "no answer choices", and missed words return later ([Duolingo blog, Oct 2026](https://blog.duolingo.com/october-2026-update/)). The same company admits that answering from a word bank and typing "require very different levels of active recall" ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-how-duolingo-teaches-chinese-and-other-languages)). The main criticism of tap-only apps is that they never ask learners to produce a word ([Mezzoguild](https://www.mezzoguild.com/drops-review/)).

## Fun comes from what players must attend to, not from timers and coins

The design principle with the best evidence is **intrinsic integration**: the learning content *is* the core mechanic, not a quiz gate in front of unrelated play. In the original study, children learned more from the integrated version of a maths game and chose to play it **about 7× longer** in free play ([Habgood & Ainsworth 2011](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/23577510/motivating-children-to-learn-effectively-exploring-the-value-of-intrinsic-integration-in-educational-games)). A pre-registered study (n = 210) placed the mechanism in attention rather than motivation: "players attend only to features needed for the game task" ([Cutting & Iacovides 2022](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/189921/)). For flipword this becomes a test for every game: *what must the player attend to in order to win?* If the answer is the meaning-to-form link, the game teaches. If the player can win on word length, tile position, cognates or tapping speed, those are what get learned. The test explains why Match and Unscramble leak value and Spell it doesn't. It also says a typing shooter teaches only if the falling object is the *meaning* and the typed text is the *target word*.

Speed is the most common trap. Typing speed varies from roughly **20 to 110 WPM**, so timing-based challenges punish slow typists and encourage gaming the timer. A typing-game designer recommends making accuracy, consistency and spelling the challenge instead ([Bailly, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/finding-gameplay-depth-in-typing-games)). Classroom platforms show the same failure at scale. Teachers criticize popular modes as "very luck based", with students chasing first place rather than answering ([Common Sense Education](https://www.commonsense.org/education/reviews/blooket/teacher-reviews/5092501)). The modes teachers credit with learning resurface missed questions ([Differentiated Teaching](https://www.differentiatedteaching.com/gimkit/)).

Rewards should inform, not pay. A meta-analysis of 128 experiments concluded that expected, tangible, task-contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, though that conclusion has been contested ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)). Gamification overall has a small-to-medium effect on cognitive outcomes (**g = 0.49**). Its effects on motivation and behaviour are less stable, and combining competition with collaboration helps behaviour most ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)). "Juice" (responsive feedback animation and sound) raised players' sense of competence in one study where gamification elements did not. That result is verified only at abstract level ([Hicks et al., Staffordshire eprint](https://eprints.staffs.ac.uk/8527/1/3677084.pdf)). The practical reading is to make every effect carry information about the answer. A lock-in animation and TTS on a correct recall, a shake plus the correct form on a miss, a visible streak meter and a new personal best all qualify. Coin shops and cosmetic currencies do not.

Social features need care. Leaderboards with strangers raised one app's total learning time by **17%** and tripled its highly engaged learners. A friends-only board "wasn't particularly effective", and an elaborate in-lesson moves counter was "completely neutral" ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth)). These are company self-reports. Low-rank positions demotivate weaker learners ([Internet Research 2023](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/intr-12-2021-0897/full/html)). For a single-user PWA with no matched player pool, personal-best ghosts and seeded "beat my score" links give the tension without the low-rank harm.

The hardest market signal cuts against the learning research. Quizlet removed Gravity, its typed falling-word mode, because "it wasn't used as much as other study modes", and kept its tap-based modes ([BFA Mercury](https://bfamercury.org/6555/news/quizlets-shift-to-quizlet-plus/)). Recall-heavy tools are routinely described as effective but boring ([LingoLegend](https://www.lingolegend.com/post/need-a-fun-alternative-to-flashcard-apps)). Learners voluntarily play what is easy, and learn from what is effortful. flipword should not assume a typing arcade game will be popular just because it teaches well. It should keep tap-based games as warm-ups, add hint ladders so recall feels achievable, and watch replay rates per game.

## The browser can deliver most of this once three repo issues are fixed

Most recommended games need no new infrastructure. Cloze and sentence games run client-side. `Intl.Segmenter` is Baseline 2024 and splits Japanese, Chinese and Thai into word segments where splitting on spaces fails ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Global_Objects/Intl/Segmenter)). A tiered matcher (exact token, then case and diacritic-insensitive, then a prefix or edit-distance stem match for spaced languages) can find the target word in user-written examples. When nothing matches, the game skips the card rather than blanking the wrong word. Irregular forms (go/went), German separable verbs and Japanese conjugations defeat string matching. They need a stored marked span, which an LLM pass can produce. Picture games are technically trivial because photos are served same-origin from R2. Their weakness is coverage, because many cards have no photo.

Audio is where the code needs work first.

**Issue 1: voice matching in `src/lib/speech.ts`.** `pickVoice()` compares `v.lang.toLowerCase().split("-")[0]` with the base language. Android Chrome and Samsung report underscore codes (`en_us`), and Firefox for Android reports three-letter codes (`eng-US-f000`), so the base-language fallback never matches on those platforms ([Readium](https://readium.org/speech/docs/WebSpeech.html)). The function also reads `getVoices()` synchronously without waiting for `voiceschanged`, so the first `speak()` can run with no voice selected. More broadly, Android Chrome lists voices whose packs are not installed and silently falls back to English, Android Edge returns an empty list, and Safari marks every voice as `default` ([Readium](https://readium.org/speech/docs/WebSpeech.html)). The fix is to normalize `_` to `-`, map ISO 639-2/3 codes to 639-1, and await the voice list. A `hasVoiceFor(lang)` helper should then gate audio-only games. Audio-only games should also include a "sounds wrong? hide audio games" escape hatch, because the Android fallback cannot be detected. If voice gaps prove common for a language pair, Workers AI MeloTTS costs about **$0.0002 per audio minute**. Generating each word once and caching it in R2 is negligible in cost ([Cloudflare pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers-ai/platform/pricing/)), though its language coverage was not verified.

**Issue 2: the CSP has no `media-src`.** `public/_headers` falls back to `default-src 'self'`. That is fine for same-origin server TTS. Playing back a learner's own recording from a `blob:` URL, which the record-and-compare mode of Out Loud needs, requires `media-src 'self' blob:`.

**Issue 3 (found while reviewing the repo; not in the feasibility notes): `Permissions-Policy: microphone=()`.** The same `_headers` file disables the microphone for the app's own origin. Any speech-recognition or recording feature would be blocked until this becomes `microphone=(self)`. The self-check version of Out Loud needs no microphone, which is one reason it comes first.

Speech recognition is a progressive enhancement, not a foundation. MDN still lists `SpeechRecognition` as not Baseline. Some browsers send audio to a server unless `processLocally` is set ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/SpeechRecognition)). Chrome has supported on-device recognition since version 139 ([Chrome AI dev preview](https://groups.google.com/a/chromium.org/g/chrome-ai-dev-preview-discuss/c/Rlyc2uAsFCY)), and Firefox 160 shipped on-device recognition on desktop for about 40 languages, a detail confirmed only from search snippets ([Phoronix](https://phoronix.com/news/Firefox-160-Released)). iOS and Firefox for Android remain unreliable or unsupported. Workers AI Whisper works in every browser through a MediaRecorder upload, at about **$0.0005 per audio minute** ([Cloudflare pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers-ai/platform/pricing/)), and keeps audio inside flipword's own infrastructure. Single accented words are hard for general-purpose recognizers. Grading should therefore be lenient (accept any of several alternatives within a small edit distance) and framed as "recognized / not recognized", never as a pronunciation score.

AI-generated content is the optional unlock for cards without example sentences. At roughly **$0.07 per 1,000 words** on an 8B model, the free Workers AI tier covers about 1,500 words a day ([Cloudflare pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers-ai/platform/pricing/)). Quality is the real risk, not cost: wrong word sense, unnatural sentences in low-resource languages. Generated sentences should be opt-in, include the card's back to fix the sense, return a validated marked span, carry an "AI" badge, be cached per word and language pair in D1, and stay editable. None of the Tier 1 games depends on AI content.

One plumbing change matters for every data-gated game. `/decks/:id/play` returns `count` random cards, and `GAME_CATALOG.usable` filters them on the client. A deck of 500 cards with 20 photos would hand Snapshot about one usable card per 30 fetched. The play endpoint needs a server-side filter, for example `?needs=image|example`, plus a weighting toward due and weak cards (see below).

## Ranked games to build, in three tiers

Evidence strength uses three levels. **Strong** means meta-analytic or replicated controlled evidence. **Moderate** means several controlled studies, some mixed. **Weak** means frameworks, design reasoning or descriptive reports only. Effort is **S** (days, mostly reuses existing components), **M** (one to two weeks, new logic or UI) or **L** (several weeks or new infrastructure). The FSRS column refers to the opt-in policy described in the cross-cutting section.

| Rank | Game | Tier | Core mechanism | Evidence | Needs | Effort | FSRS signal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gap Line | Build first | Cloze recall in context (evaluation) | Moderate | Example sentence | M | Typed mode only |
| 2 | Echo | Build first | Audio-cued recall and dictation | Moderate (mechanism) / Weak (format) | Target-language TTS voice | S–M | Typed dictation only |
| 3 | Snapshot | Build first | Picture-cued productive recall | Moderate | Photo | S | Typed mode only |
| 4 | Out Loud | Build first | Spoken recall (production effect) | Moderate | Photo or meaning; TTS optional | S (self-check) / L (ASR) | ASR-verified only |
| 5 | Showdown | Build next | Recognition → letters → recall ladder on weakest words | Moderate | Front and back | M | Typed phase only |
| 6 | Downpour | Build next | Typed recall inside an arcade loop | Strong (mechanism) / unproven wrapper | Front and back; photo optional | M–L | Yes, typed |
| 7 | Letter Logic | Build next | Guided production with letter feedback | Weak | Front, short alphabetic words | M | No |
| 8 | Grid Clues | Later | Crossword cued recall | Weak | Front and back | L | No |
| 9 | Line Up | Later | Reorder the example sentence | Weak | Example sentence | M | No |
| 10 | Lane Dash | Experimental | Recognition under motion, for fluency | Weak | Front and back | M | No |
| 11 | Expedition | Experimental | Roguelite run that chains other games as rooms | None direct | Any | L | Inherits from rooms |

### Tier 1: build first

#### 1. Gap Line (sentence-gap recall)

**How it plays.** The card's example sentence appears with the target word blanked out and the meaning shown as a small hint. The learner fills the gap, by picking from four deck words while the card is new or weak and by typing it once the card is established. A correct answer completes the sentence and TTS reads it aloud. **Learning mechanism.** Retrieval plus evaluation of fit plus a varied encounter. Evaluation is the strongest involvement component ([Yanagisawa & Webb 2021](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/lang.12444)), but fill-in-the-blank alone gains less than flashcard recall, so the typed mode is the real payoff. Evidence is **Moderate**. **Engagement hook.** Each answer completes a real sentence, a small "aha" that word-only games lack, and the spoken full sentence is informative juice. **Data needed.** An example sentence in which the matcher can find the front. Cards without one, or whose sentence doesn't match, are skipped. The game hides itself when fewer than 4 usable cards exist (MC needs three distractors). Later, opt-in AI-generated sentences with marked spans can fill coverage. **Feasibility.** **M**: the tiered `Intl.Segmenter` matcher, MC distractors that are other deck fronts of similar shape, and reuse of Spell it's typed input and `DiffText`. **Risks.** The matcher can blank the wrong token in inflected languages (mitigation: skip on no confident match). The sentence can admit another valid deck word (mitigation: accept any deck front that fits exactly, or show the meaning hint). Some cloze apps draw complaints about inaccurate sentences, so this game should stick to user-written sentences first ([Langoly](https://www.langoly.com/clozemaster-review/)).

#### 2. Echo (listen, then recall)

**How it plays.** The word is spoken with no text shown. In the first stage the learner picks its meaning from four options. When the card is stronger, the learner types the word they heard (dictation). A replay button and a slow-replay button are always available, and a wrong answer reveals the written word while it plays again. **Learning mechanism.** Audio-cued receptive recall and sound-to-spelling mapping. These train the phonological form, which flipword currently ignores. Retrieval itself is **Strong**; listening-specific formats are **Weak** because no meta-analysis of dictation was found. Overall evidence is **Moderate**. **Engagement hook.** A new sense channel and a clear "ear training" skill, and the round is fast with no reading. **Data needed.** Only the front, plus a target-language voice. When `hasVoiceFor(lang)` is false, the game is hidden for that deck, with a short explanation of why. A per-user "audio sounds wrong" toggle covers Android's silent English fallback. Server TTS cached in R2 is the later fallback. **Feasibility.** **S–M** after the `speech.ts` fix. The meaning-pick stage reuses `QuizGame` rendering, and dictation reuses Spell it's input. `speak()` must run in the click handler for Safari. **Risks.** Voice quality varies by OS (an iOS 18 regression reportedly downgraded web voices; [Apple forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/764438)). Homophones need to accept any deck word with the same spelling. Deaf and hard-of-hearing users need the game marked as audio-only and never required.

#### 3. Snapshot (photo → word)

**How it plays.** A card's photo fills the screen and the learner types the word. Brand-new cards get a four-option guess first, with feedback. The meaning text appears only after answering, so the photo is the cue, not decoration. **Learning mechanism.** Picture-cued productive recall, which exploits the picture advantage once retrieval removes overconfidence ([Carpenter & Olson 2012](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/is-a-picture-really-worth-a-thousand-words-evaluating-contributio/)). The guess-first stage uses the pretesting effect ([Chua & Pan 2026](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/CP_2026.pdf)). Evidence is **Moderate**: one null replication exists. **Engagement hook.** It feels like naming the world, and personal photos are memorable. **Data needed.** A photo (`imageKey`). It requires the server-side `needs=image` filter, and the game is hidden when fewer than 4 photo cards exist. The same "photo as prompt" layer should also be added to Spell it, Quiz and Downpour for cards that have one, so photos earn their keep even in decks too sparse for Snapshot. **Feasibility.** **S**: Spell it with an image prompt. **Risks.** Low coverage. Ambiguous photos, where one image could show several words, need the meaning available as a paid hint. Showing a photo *beside* the answer in other games recreates the overconfidence trap.

#### 4. Out Loud (say the word)

**How it plays.** A photo or meaning appears and the learner says the target word aloud, then taps to reveal the written word and hear it via TTS, and rates themselves (got it / close / missed). Where on-device recognition is available and the user opts in, the app listens and marks the answer recognized or not recognized. **Learning mechanism.** Spoken productive recall: the production effect, larger in L2 ([Brown & Roembke 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11111515/)), plus retrieval beating imitation ([Kang et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0450-z)). Evidence is **Moderate**, mixed for children. **Engagement hook.** The most "real-world" game, matching the market's newest direction ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/october-2026-update/)), with a skip for "can't speak now". **Data needed.** Front and back, using the photo when present. TTS is optional for the model answer, and without a voice the reveal shows text only. **Feasibility.** **S** for self-check, which needs no microphone. **L** for recognition: change `Permissions-Policy` to `microphone=(self)`, use `processLocally` with `available()`/`install()` on Chrome, Edge and Firefox desktop, and add a consent prompt for any cloud mode. The Whisper upload fallback adds `media-src 'self' blob:` for playback. **Risks.** Self-rated scores make a competitive best score meaningless, so store "words said" rather than a ranked best, and never write FSRS reviews from self-ratings. Recognizers mishear isolated learner words, so grading must be lenient. Privacy wording is required whenever audio leaves the device.

### Tier 2: build next

#### 5. Showdown (boss battle on your weakest words)

**How it plays.** A boss has health equal to about 8 of the learner's weakest words, chosen from recent misses and low FSRS stability. Phase 1 attacks with four-option picks, phase 2 with letter tiles, and phase 3 with typed recall. Correct answers hit the boss. A wrong answer lets the boss strike, reveals the answer, and requeues the word two or three turns later. **Learning mechanism.** The recognition → cued recall → free recall ladder recommended by [Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf), with recall before recognition helping later ([Smith & Karpicke 2014](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Smith_Karpicke_Memory.pdf)), spaced micro-repeats and adaptive targeting. Evidence is **Moderate**. **Engagement hook.** A dramatic arc in under three minutes and a natural end-of-session capstone. The boss is literally the learner's own weak spots, so winning means something. **Data needed.** Front and back only, plus `practice_log` history to choose targets (it falls back to random cards for new decks). **Feasibility.** **M**: it composes existing Quiz, Unscramble and Spell it logic into one stateful round and needs the weak-card selection endpoint. **Risks.** The fiction layer must stay thin so attention stays on the words. If the deck has fewer weak words than the boss's health, fill with due cards.

#### 6. Downpour (typing arcade)

**How it plays.** Meanings, and photos where available, fall slowly toward the ground, and the learner types the target word to clear one, choosing which to hit. Long words fall slowly with more "armour", short words fall faster, and a correct typed word occasionally triggers a freeze. Speed adapts to the player's measured rate, and score multiplies with accuracy streaks, not reaction time. **Learning mechanism.** Productive typed recall, fully integrated as the action ([Habgood & Ainsworth 2011](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/23577510/motivating-children-to-learn-effectively-exploring-the-value-of-intrinsic-integration-in-educational-games)). The mechanism is **Strong**; the arcade wrapper is unproven for vocabulary. **Engagement hook.** Real choice of target, rising tension, juice on every clear, and a ghost of the personal best. Typing games gain depth from choice and archetypes ([Bailly](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/finding-gameplay-depth-in-typing-games)). **Data needed.** Front and back; photos are optional prompts. It works on any deck of 5+ cards, recycling words in small decks. **Feasibility.** **M–L**: animation loop, input targeting, mobile keyboard occlusion (on phones, fewer simultaneous drops and a fixed input above the keyboard), a `prefers-reduced-motion` static mode, and a relaxed untimed mode. **Risks.** The removal of Quizlet's Gravity mode for low usage is the direct warning ([BFA Mercury](https://bfamercury.org/6555/news/quizlets-shift-to-quizlet-plus/)). Ship it only after the accent strip exists, and measure replay rate. Speed pressure must not exclude slow typists, and screen-reader users need a turn-based path elsewhere.

#### 7. Letter Logic (deduction puzzle)

**How it plays.** The meaning is shown and the hidden answer is a deck word. The learner submits candidate words, each of which must be a deck word or a full attempt at the answer, and gets per-letter feedback (right letter and place, right letter wrong place, absent) in flipword's own visual style, within six tries. A daily seeded puzzle per deck gives a reason to return tomorrow. **Learning mechanism.** Guided production with spelling feedback, where each guess is itself a retrieval of a deck word. Evidence is **Weak**: descriptive reports that the format trains spelling and recall of rare words ([INTED 2023](https://library.iated.org/view/CONNOR2023USI)). **Engagement hook.** Deduction, a daily ritual and a shareable result pattern. **Data needed.** Front only, best for 3–8 letter alphabetic words. The game is hidden for CJK decks and for decks with fewer than about 10 qualifying words. **Feasibility.** **M**. **Risks.** Letter-strategy can replace meaning-to-form recall; showing the meaning prominently and accepting only deck words keeps the focus on meaning. Visual sameness with a famous puzzle should be avoided by design.

### Tier 3: later or experimental

**8. Grid Clues (deck crossword).** An auto-generated mini crossword with meanings, photos or blanked sentences as clues. This is cued recall without a word bank, which an expert considers supportive of vocabulary, unlike word search ([Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/blogs/sound-it-out/are-word-searches-waste-instructional-time)). Evidence is **Weak**; effort is **L** because grid generation fails on small or letter-poor decks, which then fall back to a plain clue list. It is a strong candidate for the daily-puzzle slot if Letter Logic proves popular.

**9. Line Up (rebuild the sentence).** The example sentence is split into word tiles with `Intl.Segmenter`, and the learner drags them into order, with TTS reading the finished sentence. It adds the "use" dimension, but apps that targeted form + meaning + use showed a smaller effect (**d = 0.65**) than form + meaning (**d = 1.33**) ([Zhou & Zhou 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/79C8F00E48D521ED3BCFE0625B5549F3/S0958344025100335a.pdf/metaanalysis_on_mobileassisted_vocabulary_learning_do_mobile_applications_help.pdf)). Word banks are also known to be easy ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-how-duolingo-teaches-chinese-and-other-languages)). Evidence is **Weak** and effort **M**. It needs an example sentence, skips cards without one, and should accept only the original order to keep free-word-order languages fair. Reserve it for mastered words.

**10. Lane Dash (three-lane runner).** A meaning appears overhead and the learner swipes or taps into the lane whose gate shows the matching word, with distractors of similar length. It is pure recognition under motion, so it belongs to the fluency strand and should draw only from known words. Evidence is **Weak** and effort **M**. It suits one-handed phone play and needs tap-lane controls and a reduced-motion mode.

**11. Expedition (short roguelite run).** A three-minute run through five to seven rooms. Each room is one of the games above, and between rooms the player picks a perk (extra hint, timer freeze, double points on typed answers). Perks may change *support*, never skip recall, and map themes unlock through words mastered, not grinding. There is no direct evidence; effort is **L**. Build it only once at least four room-sized games exist.

**Not recommended.** Word search, because finding letter shapes has no research support for vocabulary ([Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/blogs/sound-it-out/are-word-searches-waste-instructional-time)). Memory/concentration, because spatial memory dominates and it adds nothing Match lacks. Hangman, because letter-guessing strategy displaces recall and Unscramble already covers the easy-scaffold niche. Coin shops or currencies earned per answer, because of the overjustification risk. Live multiplayer rooms need a WebSocket service flipword doesn't have, and the evidence for them is classroom-only ([Wang & Tahir 2020](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/39435)). Revisit them only if teachers become a target audience.

## Cross-cutting fixes multiply the value of every game

**Reveal on every wrong answer.** Quiz and Spell it already show the correct answer. True or false does not: a wrong answer gets a 250 ms red flash and the next statement. On a miss it should show the true pairing ("chien = dog") with a cross icon and text, optionally speak it, and pause the 60 s clock for the reveal. Match should flash the correct partner of a wrongly picked tile. This is the cheapest learning win in the codebase, given negative suggestibility and the finding that retrieval helped children only with feedback ([van Eersel](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/105295/Proefschrift_GvEersel.pdf)). It is **S**.

**Retry missed words, inside and after the round.** `GamePage.tsx` already lists missed words under "to review". The only action, though, is "Play again", which fetches a fresh random set. Add a "Practise these N" button that replays the missed cards, defaulting to Spell it, or to Unscramble for long words, and play each card's audio in the list. Inside a round, requeue a missed word two or three items later, which gives spaced micro-repetition rather than back-to-back repeats ([Kim & Webb 2022](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/35602)). Effort is **S** and **M** respectively.

**Choose cards on purpose.** Replace `order by random()` in `worker/routes/games.ts` with a weighted draw: due and overdue cards, recent game misses from `practice_log`, then the rest. Timed games (Match, True or false, Lane Dash) should draw from cards with established stability, so speed builds fluency rather than disrupting encoding ([Benjamin & Bjork 2000](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Benjamin_RBjork_2000.pdf)). Brand-new cards should enter through a guess-first Quiz or Snapshot stage ([Chua & Pan 2026](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/CP_2026.pdf)). Add the `needs=image|example` filter. Effort is **M**.

**Let typed recall, and only typed recall, feed FSRS, on an opt-in basis.** No FSRS documentation or study validates game answers as reviews, so the policy below is inference from how FSRS models memory and from the recall-versus-recognition evidence. Keep today's default: games don't reschedule. Add a setting, "Typed game answers count as reviews". It would apply to Spell it, typed Gap Line, Echo dictation, Downpour, Showdown's typed phase, Snapshot's typed mode and ASR-verified Out Loud. It writes a review only when the card is due or overdue, only for the card's first answer that day, and never Easy. The mapping is: wrong → Again; correct after a hint, a retry or a near-miss accent → Hard; clean correct → Good. FSRS defines Hard as "remembered with serious difficulty" ([py-fsrs](https://pypi.org/project/fsrs/)). Recognition games (Quiz, Match, True or false, Unscramble, Lane Dash, guess-first stages) should never write reviews, because crediting guessable successes would push out intervals on words the learner can't produce. They should still feed signals: confusable pairs for distractor selection, leech detection, and adding a failed card to today's study queue without touching its memory state. This needs `GameAnswer` (`src/features/games/types.ts`) and the `practice_log` table to carry format, hint use, attempt number and latency, and every game-written review should carry a `source: "game:<id>"` tag so parameter optimization can exclude it later. `GameInfo.kind` should split into recognition, scaffolded and recall, because Unscramble is labelled `recall` today but gives the letters away. Effort is **M**.

**Score accuracy over speed, and offer relaxed modes.** True or false's multiplier already rewards consecutive correct answers, which is good, and it should keep that design. Match scores pure time, which favours shape-matching. Every timed game should get a relaxed or untimed mode with its own best score, which needs a mode column on `game_session`. The game should also ask "adjust the game speed" and "do not make precise timing essential" of itself ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)). Match should refill the board from a queue so the last pairs are never free by elimination. Effort is **S–M**.

**Accessibility.** True or false currently signals right and wrong by border colour alone. It should add icon and text, as Quiz already does with its check and cross icons and labels. Moving games need a `prefers-reduced-motion` static mode, large well-spaced touch targets, pause and resume for phone interruptions, and a turn-based path through every deck for screen-reader users, built on the existing `aria-live` regions. Audio must never be the only carrier of information except in Echo, which is explicitly optional ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)). Effort is **S–M**.

**Accent and script input.** `compareAnswer` in `src/features/study/typing.ts` deliberately counts accents. That is right for spelling, but painful on phone keyboards, where accents need a long-press. Add an accent strip above every typed input, built from the special characters present in the deck's fronts and inserting at the caret. Add an optional "lenient accents" setting that accepts the bare letter, shows "almost — watch the accent", scores slightly lower and maps to Hard for FSRS. `compareAnswer` also does not NFC-normalize input, so composed and decomposed forms of the same accented letter compare unequal. That is a one-line fix, observed in the repo rather than in the notes. For CJK and other IME scripts, skip grading while `isComposing` is true, and prefer tile-based games, since typed recall on phones is slow. These recommendations are design reasoning; no source on mobile accent UX was found. Effort is **S**.

**Smarter distractors.** `buildQuiz` picks random distractors. Distractors that are orthographically or semantically close make items harder and more discriminating ([Ludewig et al. 2023](https://eldorado.tu-dortmund.de/items/2f2f24a7-464f-48d5-908b-d9e7ea2a97a9)). Use easy, random distractors for new cards, to avoid the short-term interference of near-synonyms ([Kaitsu & Nakata 2025](https://www.howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Kaitsu-Nakata-2025-CALL.pdf)). Use similar-shaped and previously confused deck words for mature cards, mined from the user's own wrong picks in `practice_log`. Exclude normalized duplicates and true synonyms. Effort is **M**.

**Keep, change or demote the existing five:**

| Game | Verdict | Changes |
|---|---|---|
| Spell it | **Keep and promote** as the core recall game | Graded hints (letter count, first letter, audio), each downgrading the FSRS signal to Hard; photo and audio prompt variants; accent strip |
| Quiz | **Keep, reposition** as the on-ramp and guess-first introduction for new words | Shape-matched and confusable distractors; photo→word and audio→meaning variants; feeds the ladder |
| Unscramble | **Keep as a scaffold** between Quiz and Spell it | Always show the meaning before the letters; relabel as scaffolded rather than recall; best for long words |
| Match | **Keep as warm-up and fluency** | Known words only when timed; refill board from a queue; photo or audio tiles; relaxed mode; reveal correct partner on a miss |
| True or false | **Demote and fix** | Reveal the true pairing on every miss; known words only; icon and text feedback; later, a sentence version ("does this word fit here?") once Gap Line's matcher exists |

## Conclusion

The research reframes what a "better game" means for flipword. The aim is not more game types. The aim is moving each word up a ladder, from recognizing it, to producing it in writing, in a sentence and out loud. The fun layer then rewards that climb with feedback the learner can feel. Most of the ladder's missing rungs (context, sound, picture, speech) are already sitting in flipword's card data or the browser, which makes the Tier 1 games unusually cheap for their learning value. Every one of them uses the same pattern: the thing the player must attend to in order to win is the meaning-to-form link.

The unresolved tension is engagement. The evidence says the effortful games teach and the easy ones get played, and no study tells flipword how to close that gap for its users. flipword already logs every game answer per word, so it is positioned to generate the evidence the literature lacks. It can compare next-review recall after game-logged and flashcard-logged answers, measure whether recognition successes predict later typed recall, and track replay rates for Downpour against Match. Those measurements should decide whether recognition games ever earn an FSRS signal and whether the arcade tier is worth expanding. That loop of instrument, compare and adjust is a sounder long-term bet than any single game on this list.
